How to read these diagrams

Four shapes, used the same way in every figure. Processes are ours and carry a level number; entities sit outside the system boundary; stores are tables in the medcode PostgreSQL database unless marked in‑memory. A corner tick means the same entity or store is drawn more than once in that figure, to keep lines short.

Colour is doubled by shape, never load‑bearing on its own. The single exception worth knowing: vermilion marks flows that write to the hospital — the one direction in this system that changes a record we do not own.

Figure 1 · Level 0

Context — the system boundary

Two kinds of human above, two machines below. The backend owns its database outright, so no store appears at this level: everything crossing a line here crosses a process boundary too.

Figure 2 · Level 1

The case coding pipeline

Read top to bottom and it is a case's life: pulled from the hospital, shown to a coder, annotated by a machine, ticked by a human, sent back. Each row shows a process, the stores it leans on, and the party on the other end of the wire.

Only the primary stores are drawn. The complete process × table map lives in Figure 10.

Figure 3 · Level 1

Identity, administration, knowledge and analytics

The other six processes. Three of them (8.0, 10.0, and part of 11.0) are mostly conduits — they exist so the browser never speaks to the RAG service or the hospital directly, and so a token check happens before anything downstream is touched.

Figure 4 · Level 2 of 2.0

Sync — pulling a visit out of the hospital

The batch prologue runs once; everything from the gate down runs once per visit, each in its own transaction so one bad case cannot roll back the batch. The gate is the whole safety story: a case a coder has already finalized is never re-written by a sync.

Figure 5 · Level 2 of 6.1

Approve — two phases, one lock

This is the diagram to read before changing anything in ApprovalService. Our database commits first; the hospital calls are queued during the transaction and only sent afterwards, still holding the write lock. That ordering is deliberate, and it has a consequence worth staring at: the two sides can disagree.

Ordering is load-bearing Cancel, then update, then create. A code moved from one visit row to another would collide with the hospital's per-visit uniqueness rule (409) if the create ran before the cancel of the row it replaces.

Figure 6 · Level 2 of 6.6

Reopen — replaying the journal backwards

Reopen is not an undo log applied blindly. Each journal entry is checked against the hospital's current rows before its inverse is sent, so a reopen after someone else edited the visit does the least damage it can.

Figure 7 · Level 2 of 1.0

Authentication — minting a token, and spending it

Two separate flows share one store. The left runs once per login; the right runs on every guarded request. What crosses between them is a hash, never the token itself.

Figure 8 · Level 2 of 5.0, 8.0, 10.0

The RAG seam — four flows, one service

Everything AI-shaped in this product goes through one FastAPI service that has no authentication of its own. Three of these four flows are pass-throughs; the fourth, sync-codes, is the odd one out — it reads what the index already holds and writes our own catalogs.

Figure 9 · Cross-cutting

One writer, one cache

Two in-process mechanisms shape every flow above, and both are the reason this backend must never be scaled past one instance. The negative fact is as important as the positive ones: saving working state deliberately does not invalidate the cache.

Three rows worth a second look

document_chunks is written by every sync and read by nothing — it was populated for a retrieval path the RAG service ended up owning instead. knowledge_base is touched only by the demo baseline; nothing in the running system writes it. And users is the only table 11.0 does not truncate: accounts and their live sessions survive a demo reset, with a deleted baseline account re-inserted purely so the restored audit rows still have something to point at.
